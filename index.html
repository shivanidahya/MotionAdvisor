AI Drafting Advisor

How should AI help with this motion?

Work through the tradeoff for the specific motion in front of you: frame it, apply the hard stops, weigh the options against what matters to you, and record your own choice. The tool shows its reasoning at every step and doesn’t decide for you.

Cell

How this works

A transparent way to weigh a hard call

The AI Drafting Advisor is a choice matrix: options in columns, the things you care about in rows, a weight on each row and a rating in each cell. Hard stops come first, the weighing comes second, and the decision stays with you. No AI model runs in the tool, so the same answers always produce the same result.

  1. Stage 1Frame

    Multiple-choice answers about the motion, the AI tool, what would go into it and your safeguards.

  2. Stage 2Gates

    Hard stops are checked before anything is weighed.

    Blocked options leave the matrix
  3. Stage 3Weigh

    Your weights × the ratings give each open option a score from 0 to 100.

  4. Stage 3Stress-test

    Sensitivity, an optional uncertainty panel and short bias checks.

  5. Stage 4Decide

    You pick any open option, say why, and get a decision record.

1. Frame the decision

You describe the court, the motion, the deadline, the AI tool and its data settings, what information would go into it, and how citations and the final draft will be checked. Every question is multiple choice, so there is nowhere to type client details. Your answers stay in this browser tab and are cleared when you close it.

Choosing a motion suggests whether it is dispositive; choosing a tool type suggests its data settings. Both are only starting points to confirm.

2. Gates come before weighing

Some risks aren’t tradeoffs. If confidential information would go into a tool that may train on or keep it, if citations won’t all be verified, if AI would draft sworn facts, if no one reviews the final motion, or if the court restricts AI, the options that depend on that risk are blocked.

A blocked option is greyed out with a lock. No weight or rating can reopen it. The only way through is to change the answer that triggered the gate, for example by committing to check every citation.

3. Weigh: weights × ratings

Each factor gets a weight from 0 (doesn’t matter to you) to 10 (matters most). Each cell gets a rating from 1 to 5, where 5 is always best for you on that factor, so for a risk factor 5 means least risk. The matrix starts with suggested weights and ratings drawn from your Stage 1 answers; selecting a cell shows exactly why it has its rating and which rule it rests on. You can change anything, and your changes are marked.

Option score = Σ (weight × rating) ÷ (Σ weights × 5) × 100

4. Close calls and sensitivity

When the top two options are less than 5 points apart, the tool says so: the numbers can’t separate them, so your judgment should. The sensitivity list tests every weight from 0 to 10, one at a time, and tells you the smallest change that would put a different option on top, for example “If you raised the weight on Time saved to 8 or more, Option D would overtake Option B.”

5. Uncertainty, kept simple

Two optional sliders: the chance an AI-generated citation error reaches the filing despite your review, and the chance the judge requires AI disclosure. Each lowers the ratings it affects, in proportion to how much AI content an option puts in the filing:

Accuracy − exposure × (error chance ÷ 10)

Compliance − disclosure burden × (disclosure chance ÷ 100)

Exposure is 0 for No AI, 0.1 for low-risk parts, 0.5 for research only, 1 for an assisted memo and 1.5 for substantial drafting. The tool then reruns the ranking across 27 combinations and tells you where the leader drops.

6. Bias checks

Short, dismissible prompts appear only when a pattern suggests a common judgment trap:

  • Automation bias: you rate an AI option’s accuracy 4 or 5 above the suggestion.
  • Deadline pressure: the motion is due in under 48 hours.
  • Overconfidence: three or more of your changes lean in AI’s favor.
  • Anchoring: “Rate without suggestions” hides every suggestion, and the ranking, until you’ve rated each cell yourself.

7. Decide and record

Stage 4 shows the top option, the runner-up and the margin. You choose any open option, including one that isn’t first, and write one or two sentences on why. The decision record keeps the framing, gates, weights, ratings, ranking, your choice and rationale, and a checklist tailored to the option you chose. Download it as a PDF or print it.

What the tool deliberately doesn’t do

  • It doesn’t decide. It ranks options by your weights; you make and record the choice.
  • It doesn’t read your draft, your client’s facts or the AI tool’s terms. It relies on your answers.
  • It doesn’t check whether a court has an AI order; it asks you whether you checked.
  • It doesn’t verify citations. Use the Citation Toolkit and a citator for that.
  • It isn’t legal advice, and a high score never replaces final review by the signing attorney.

Where the numbers live

Every option, factor, default weight, suggested rating, adjustment, gate, uncertainty setting, bias trigger and checklist item lives in one file, config.js ↗, with a comment citing the rule or reasoning behind each value. A separate file, engine.js, applies them, and automated tests check the results.

The values are judgment calls by the developer, not measured data, and are marked for review by a practising attorney. Reviewed November 2026.

Motions 101

What is a motion?

On this page
The basics

Motions in brief

A motion is a formal request asking a judge to make a specific ruling or order. It is how parties ask the court to act between the complaint and the final judgment, and sometimes after. Because a motion is signed under Rule 11, every factual claim and legal citation in it is the signing attorney’s responsibility, and that is where AI risk lands.

A motion names the relief wanted, gives the legal grounds for it and is supported by facts in the record, usually through affidavits, declarations or exhibits. The other side gets a chance to oppose it in writing, the moving party may reply, and the judge decides with or without a hearing.

Motions can be dispositive, ending all or part of the case (dismissal, summary judgment), or non-dispositive, shaping how the case proceeds (discovery, scheduling, evidence). In Massachusetts Superior Court, most civil motions follow Superior Court Rule 9A: you serve the motion on the other side first, they serve an opposition, and the moving party files the whole package. In federal court in Massachusetts, Local Rule 7.1 requires you to confer with opposing counsel and certify that you did.

About

What is Motion Advisor?

Motion Advisor helps small-firm and solo practitioners decide whether, and how, to use generative AI when drafting a legal motion, and then check the work before it is filed.

What the platform does

  • AI Drafting Advisor. A four-stage choice matrix: frame the decision, apply the hard stops, weigh five ways of using AI against eight factors with your own weights, and record the option you choose and why, with a tailored checklist, as a PDF.
  • Citation Toolkit. Finds case, statute and rule citations in a pasted or uploaded draft, flags format problems and citations already exposed as AI fabrications, and tracks your verification. Export to Excel, Google Sheets, CSV or PDF.
  • Redaction Game. Three practice rounds on what must come out of a court filing or an AI prompt.
  • Motions 101. What a motion is, its parts, its life from draft to order, and a library of 20 common motions.

How the advisor helps you decide

The advisor treats “should AI help with this motion?” as a choice among five options, from no AI to substantial AI drafting. Gates block options that carry a risk no benefit can offset, such as confidential data in a tool that keeps it or citations nobody will verify. The open options are then scored against eight factors using weights you set and ratings you can change, and the tool shows the ranking, the margin, what would change the result and how uncertainty shifts it.

You make the final choice and record your reasoning. No AI model runs in the tool, and every number comes from one commented configuration file. Read how it works.

Where the legal sources come from

Every explanation traces to a primary source: the ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct and ABA Formal Opinion 512; the Massachusetts Rules of Professional Conduct and court rules published by the Supreme Judicial Court and Trial Court on mass.gov; the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure; the Local Rules of the U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts; and published decisions on AI-fabricated citations.

Each source is listed with what it requires, where the site relies on it, and a link to the official text. Federal and Massachusetts civil-rule citations open on courtrules.net; the official Massachusetts text is published in the Massachusetts Rules of Civil Procedure on Mass.gov ↗.
