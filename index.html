How this works

How Motion Advisor calculates your risk

Motion Advisor helps you decide whether it’s worth using AI to draft your motion. Complete a short assessment, and Motion Advisor will calculate the risks and give you a report with those risks and suggestions for using AI safely. No AI model runs in the tool, so the same answers always produce the same report.

  1. Step 1Questions

    Seven multiple-choice questions about the court, the motion, the AI tool, your information, the parts of the motion and your safeguards.

  2. Step 2Hard stops

    Uses of AI that aren’t safe as planned are marked “Not recommended”.

    No score can override them
  3. Step 3Risk points

    Each part of the motion you selected gets starting points plus points for every risk factor that applies to it.

  4. Step 4Risk level

    Points become Low, Moderate or High, for each part and overall.

  5. Step 5Your Report

    Risks identified, a part-by-part table, suggestions, what would lower your risk and a checklist.

1. The questionnaire

One question per screen, with a short “Why we ask this” note on each. Every answer is multiple choice, so there is nowhere to type client details, and you can go back and change any answer before you see the report. Your answers stay in this browser tab and are cleared when you close it.

When you’re not sure about a tool’s data settings or grounding, “Not sure” is treated as the riskier answer.

2. Hard stops come first

Some risks aren’t tradeoffs. If one applies to a part of the motion you selected, that part is “Not recommended” and the overall risk is High, whatever else you answered.

3. Risk points for each part

Every part of the motion starts with a few points, because some parts carry more risk on their own: a certificate of service is routine, while the legal argument carries the authority. Each risk factor in your answers then adds points to the parts it affects.

Part points = starting points + points for each risk factor that applies to that part

A part with or more points is Moderate risk, or more is High, and anything lower is Low.

4. The overall risk level

The overall level is the highest level among the parts you selected, or High if any part hits a hard stop.

Gaps in your safeguards add points of their own. When they reach or more, the overall level goes up one step:

5. Your Report

The report opens with the overall risk level in a color-coded card that always carries a text label. It then lists each risk in plain language with the answer that triggered it and the rule behind it, and shows a part-by-part table that links to Motions 101.

After that come tailored suggestions for using AI, the one or two answer changes that would lower your risk most, and a “Before you file” checklist. Download it as a PDF or print it.

6. Compare Your Options (optional)

For a deeper look, Compare Your Options lays five ways of using AI side by side, from no AI to substantial drafting. It rates each against eight factors, and you choose how much each factor matters. It fills in from your answers and locks the options a hard stop rules out. It also shows what would change the ranking, an optional uncertainty panel and short bias checks.

What the tool deliberately doesn’t do

  • It doesn’t decide for you. The report supports your professional judgment.
  • It doesn’t read your draft, your client’s facts or the AI tool’s terms. It relies on your answers.
  • It doesn’t check whether a court has an AI order; the checklist reminds you to.
  • It doesn’t verify citations. Use the Citation Toolkit and a citator for that.
  • It isn’t legal advice, and a Low risk level never replaces final review by the signing attorney.

Where the numbers live

Every question, hard stop, risk factor, point value, threshold and suggestion lives in one file, config.js ↗, with a comment citing the rule or reasoning behind it. The tables on this page are drawn from that file, so they always match the tool.

The values are judgment calls by the developer, not measured data, and are marked for review by a practising attorney.

Redaction Game

What has to come out?

Click any word or phrase to black it out. Click again to undo. Redact every person’s first, middle and last name, plus anything the round’s rule protects. Roles like plaintiff, defendant, affiant and client stay. When you think the document is safe, submit it. Hits score +10, misses −5 and over-redactions −3, because hiding what the court needs is also a mistake.

Motions 101

What is a motion?

On this page
The basics

Motions in brief

A motion is a formal request asking a judge to make a specific ruling or order. It is how parties ask the court to act between the complaint and the final judgment, and sometimes after. Because a motion is signed under Rule 11, every factual claim and legal citation in it is the signing attorney’s responsibility, and that is where AI risk lands.

A motion names the relief wanted, gives the legal grounds for it and is supported by facts in the record, usually through affidavits, declarations or exhibits. The other side gets a chance to oppose it in writing, the moving party may reply, and the judge decides with or without a hearing.

Motions can be dispositive, ending all or part of the case (dismissal, summary judgment), or non-dispositive, shaping how the case proceeds (discovery, scheduling, evidence). In Massachusetts Superior Court, most civil motions follow Superior Court Rule 9A: you serve the motion on the other side first, they serve an opposition, and the moving party files the whole package. In federal court in Massachusetts, Local Rule 7.1 requires you to confer with opposing counsel and certify that you did.

About

What is Motion Advisor?

Motion Advisor helps you decide whether it’s worth using AI to draft your motion. Complete a short assessment, and Motion Advisor will calculate the risks and give you a report with those risks and suggestions for using AI safely.

What the platform does

  • AI Drafting Advisor. A short assessment that calculates the risks of using AI on your motion and gives you a report, downloadable as a PDF, with the risks identified and suggestions for using AI safely.
  • Compare Your Options. An optional, advanced page that weighs five ways of using AI side by side, filled in from your answers.
  • Citation Toolkit. Finds case, statute and rule citations in a pasted or uploaded draft, flags format problems and citations already exposed as AI fabrications, and tracks your verification. Export to Excel, Google Sheets, CSV or PDF.
  • Redaction Game. Three practice rounds on what must come out of a court filing or an AI prompt.
  • Motions 101. What a motion is, its parts, its life from draft to order, and a library of 20 common motions.

How the advisor works out your risk

Seven multiple-choice questions cover the court, the motion, the AI tool, what information would go into it, which parts of the motion AI would help with, how the work will be checked, and your safeguards. Hard stops come first: confidential information in a tool that may keep it, citations nobody will check, or AI-drafted sworn statements make that use “Not recommended”. Each part of the motion then gets risk points from your answers, which become a Low, Moderate or High level.

Your report explains every risk, links the rule behind it, and suggests safer ways to use AI. For a deeper look, the optional Compare Your Options page weighs different ways of using AI side by side. No AI model runs in the tool, and every rule comes from one commented configuration file. Read how it works.

Every explanation traces to a primary source: the ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct and ABA Formal Opinion 512; the Massachusetts Rules of Professional Conduct and court rules published by the Supreme Judicial Court and Trial Court on mass.gov; the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure; the Local Rules of the U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts; and published decisions on AI-fabricated citations.

Each source is listed with what it requires, where the site relies on it, and a link to the official text. Federal and Massachusetts civil-rule citations open on courtrules.net; the official Massachusetts text is published in the Massachusetts Rules of Civil Procedure on Mass.gov ↗.
